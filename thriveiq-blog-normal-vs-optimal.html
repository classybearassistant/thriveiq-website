Your Labs Are "Normal." So Why Do You Feel Terrible?

Educational content only. Not medical advice. Talk to your doctor about what's right for you.

This is the question that sent me down a rabbit hole I'm still in.

You get your labs back. Everything is normal. Your doctor seems satisfied. And yet you're exhausted, your hair is falling out, you can't think clearly, and you're gaining weight despite doing everything right.

Here's what I figured out: "normal" and "optimal" are not the same thing — and most people have no idea there's a difference.

What "normal" actually means

Conventional lab ranges are built from population averages. They're designed to catch disease — not to identify the gap between feeling sick and feeling genuinely well.

The way I think about it: "normal" is the speed limit. It tells you the legal boundary. It doesn't tell you the optimal speed for your particular car, your tires, your weather, and your destination.

Your labs being in range means you're not in a medical crisis. It doesn't mean you're operating at your best.

Where the gap shows up

Ferritin

Conventional normal: 12–150 ng/mL  ·  Functional optimal for women: 50–100 ng/mL

A ferritin of 14 is "normal." Most women feel exhausted and lose hair below 50.

TSH (Thyroid)

Conventional normal: 0.5–4.5 mIU/L  ·  Functional optimal: 1.0–2.0 mIU/L

A TSH of 4.2 is "normal." Many people feel cold, fatigued, and foggy at that level.

Fasting Glucose

Conventional normal: below 100 mg/dL  ·  Functional optimal: 70–85 mg/dL

A fasting glucose of 97 is "normal." At that level, insulin dysregulation may already be developing.

Vitamin D

Conventional normal: above 20 ng/mL  ·  Functional optimal: 50–80 ng/mL

A Vitamin D of 22 is "normal." Most people don't feel the benefits until they're above 50.

B12

Conventional normal: above 200 pg/mL  ·  Functional optimal: above 500 pg/mL

A B12 of 250 is "normal." Neurological symptoms and fatigue are common below 500.

This isn't about doctors being wrong

I want to be clear about this — because it's easy to read this kind of information and feel frustrated with your doctor. Conventional ranges exist for a reason. They're validated, standardized, and appropriate for catching disease.

Functional ranges are a different lens — one focused on how you feel, not just whether you're sick. Neither is wrong. They're answering different questions. The question worth asking isn't just "is my lab normal?" It's "where in the range am I — and could that explain how I feel?"

What I'd ask at my next appointment

  • Can you tell me the actual number, not just whether it's in range?
  • Where in the range am I — high normal, low normal, or middle?
  • Could any of my symptoms be explained by values that are technically normal but on the lower end?

Your labs being normal is good news. It's just not the whole story.
