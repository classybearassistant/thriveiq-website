Homocysteine: The Cardiovascular Marker Your Doctor Probably Didn't Check

Educational content only. Not medical advice. Talk to your doctor about what's right for you.

I stumbled onto homocysteine while researching something else entirely — and I couldn't believe it wasn't on my standard panel.

Your cholesterol looks fine. Your blood pressure is normal. But there's a marker that's strongly associated with cardiovascular risk, cognitive decline, and inflammation — and most standard panels don't include it.

What it is

Homocysteine is an amino acid that builds up in your blood when certain B vitamins are low or when a genetic variant called MTHFR impairs their processing. In a body that's methylating well, it gets converted quickly. When that conversion is impaired — from low B12, low folate, low B6, or an MTHFR variant — it accumulates.

Elevated homocysteine is associated with increased cardiovascular risk, stroke risk, cognitive decline, and inflammation — independently of cholesterol. You can have a perfect cholesterol panel and elevated homocysteine at the same time.

Why it's worth knowing about

What I find most interesting about homocysteine is that it's a downstream signal. When it's elevated, it's telling you something upstream isn't working — usually a B vitamin deficiency or impaired methylation. That makes it one of the most actionable markers out there. Address the upstream cause and homocysteine typically comes down.

The most common upstream causes are low B12, low folate, low B6, and MTHFR variants. If you've read the MTHFR article, you'll recognize why synthetic folic acid in the American food supply makes this more complicated for a lot of people.

What the numbers mean

Below 7 μmol/LOptimal
7–9 μmol/LBorderline, worth monitoring
Above 10 μmol/LElevated, worth a conversation with your doctor
Above 15 μmol/LSignificantly elevated, warrants investigation

Conventional labs often only flag it above 15. Functional medicine practitioners tend to aim for below 7–9 based on research associating lower levels with reduced cardiovascular and cognitive risk.

Who should ask about it

Worth bringing up if you have a family history of cardiovascular disease or dementia, unexplained fatigue or brain fog, known B12 or folate issues, an MTHFR variant, or elevated inflammation markers without a clear cause.

What I'd ask at my next appointment

  • Can we add homocysteine to my next panel?
  • If it's elevated, what's the most likely cause in my situation?
  • Should we look at B12, folate, and B6 alongside it?
  • Is MTHFR testing worth considering given my history?

Homocysteine is one of those markers where knowing your number genuinely changes the conversation. And it's so simple to check.
