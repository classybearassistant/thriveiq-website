That Nightly Urge to Move Your Legs Might Be a Ferritin Problem, Not a Sleep Problem

Educational content only. Not medical advice. Talk to your doctor about what's right for you.

You lie down. Within minutes, your legs start crawling, aching, pulling — some uncomfortable feeling that only eases when you move them. So you get up, pace, shake it out. By the time it settles, you've lost half an hour of sleep you'll never get back.

If this sounds familiar, here's what's worth knowing: restless legs is frequently tied to something measurable in your blood — and it's not usually the marker people assume.

It's not "just restless legs"

Restless legs syndrome (also called Willis-Ekbom disease) is a real neurological pattern, not a vague description of fidgety nights. The defining features:

  • An urge to move the legs, usually with an uncomfortable sensation attached
  • Symptoms that start or worsen when you're still
  • Relief with movement — walking or stretching
  • Symptoms that are worse in the evening or at night

If that's a familiar shape, it's worth naming out loud to your doctor rather than writing off as "just how you sleep."

The connections worth knowing about

Ferritin — the one with real research behind it

Iron plays a role in dopamine production, and dopamine signaling is tied to restless legs symptoms. Multiple studies have found restless legs symptoms are associated with low iron status — and this holds even in people whose standard bloodwork would be called "normal."

Research has specifically looked at serum ferritin as a marker worth watching, with treatment trials showing iron supplementation can meaningfully reduce symptom severity in people with low-normal ferritin, not just those who are outright anemic.

Magnesium — the evidence here is genuinely mixed

You'll see magnesium recommended everywhere for restless legs, and it's worth being honest about where the science actually stands: a few small trials found magnesium supplementation reduced symptom severity, particularly in people with confirmed low magnesium.

But a broader review of the existing research concluded there isn't strong evidence supporting magnesium specifically — iron is the mineral with the more consistent research behind it. Magnesium may still be worth logging and discussing with your doctor, but it shouldn't be assumed to be the answer the way iron status often is.

What if your ferritin already came back "normal"?

Standard lab ranges for ferritin are wide — often starting around 12–15 ng/mL for women. But the restless-legs research consistently points to a higher threshold, generally in the 50–75 ng/mL range, before symptoms tend to improve with iron treatment. A conventionally "normal" ferritin result does not rule this out.

This isn't your doctor being wrong — standard ranges are built to catch iron-deficiency anemia, not restless legs. It's a different question, and it's worth asking directly: "My ferritin came back at [your number] — is that high enough for restless legs specifically, not just for anemia?"

What to actually do with this

  • Log the pattern — 3 or more nights in a week is worth bringing to your next appointment, not something to just push through
  • Ask specifically about ferritin, not just a general iron panel — standard "iron levels normal" bloodwork doesn't always include it
  • Track it alongside your sleep quality — a pattern of poor sleep on the same nights restless legs shows up is worth surfacing

Not medical advice — always discuss significant or persistent restless legs symptoms with your healthcare provider.
