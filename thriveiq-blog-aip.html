What Is AIP and Why It Changed Everything For My Inflammation

Educational content only. Not medical advice. Talk to your doctor about what's right for you.

In 2013 my doctors were running every test they could think of — MS, lupus, rheumatoid arthritis. They were looking for autoimmune disease because my symptoms pointed that way. Everything came back normal.

While they were looking, I was researching. And that's how I found the Autoimmune Protocol.

It sounded extreme at first. But when you've been dismissed enough times and the tests keep coming back normal while you keep feeling terrible, extreme starts to sound like worth trying.

I tried the elimination diet myself — not because a doctor recommended it, but because I needed to start getting answers somewhere. And what I found was that my body had a lot to say once I started listening to it through food.

What AIP actually is

AIP stands for Autoimmune Protocol. It's an elimination diet — meaning you remove specific foods for a period of time, let your body settle, and then reintroduce them one at a time to see what's actually causing your symptoms.

It's not a forever diet. It's a detective tool.

The idea behind it is that certain foods can trigger inflammation in people with autoimmune conditions or gut dysfunction — and the only way to know which foods are affecting you specifically is to remove them all and add them back methodically.

Think of it like rebooting a computer. You strip it down, let it run clean, and figure out what was causing the problems before you reload everything.

What you remove — and why

The standard AIP elimination phase removes:

  • Grains — all of them, including gluten-free grains
  • Legumes — beans, lentils, peanuts, soy
  • Dairy
  • Eggs
  • Nightshades — tomatoes, peppers, eggplant, potatoes (not sweet potatoes)
  • Nuts and seeds
  • Refined sugar and alcohol
  • NSAIDs like ibuprofen

That list looks overwhelming. I know. Stay with me.

Each of these food groups has a reason for being on the list. Grains and legumes contain compounds called lectins and phytates that can irritate the gut lining. Nightshades contain alkaloids that some people react to. Eggs — particularly egg whites — are a surprisingly common inflammatory trigger. Dairy affects gut permeability in ways that aren't fully understood yet.

The point isn't that these foods are bad for everyone. The point is that you don't know which ones are affecting you specifically until you remove them and test.

What you eat instead

This is where people get stuck — they focus on what's removed and miss what's left. AIP is actually built around some of the most nutrient-dense foods available:

  • Quality meat and fish — grass-fed, wild-caught where possible
  • Organ meats — liver especially, one of the most nutrient-dense foods on the planet
  • Vegetables — most of them, in abundance
  • Fruit — in moderation
  • Sweet potatoes and other starchy root vegetables
  • Healthy fats — olive oil, avocado oil, coconut oil
  • Bone broth
  • Fermented foods — coconut yogurt, sauerkraut, kimchi

When I shifted my focus from what I was losing to what I was actually eating, the whole thing felt less restrictive.

How long the elimination phase lasts

Most practitioners recommend 30–90 days before beginning reintroduction. The goal is to give your gut enough time to calm down and your immune system enough time to settle so that when you add foods back, you actually notice what happens.

Rushing the reintroduction is the most common mistake. If you do 30 days and start adding everything back at once, you learn nothing.

The reintroduction — where the real information lives

This is the part that actually changed things for me. Reintroduction is done one food at a time, with at least 3–5 days between each new food. You eat a small amount, then a larger amount, and you watch for symptoms over the next 72 hours.

Symptoms can be anything — joint pain, bloating, brain fog, fatigue, skin changes, changes in bowel habits, mood shifts. The timing matters too. Some reactions are immediate. Some show up 48 hours later.

What you end up with after a full reintroduction is a personal map — your body's specific response to specific foods. Not a general list of "inflammatory foods." Your list. That's the part no standard panel can give you.

What AIP isn't

AIP isn't a weight loss diet — though some people lose weight on it because they're removing a lot of processed food.

It isn't a permanent way of eating for most people — the goal is reintroduction, not lifelong restriction.

And it isn't a cure. It's information. What you do with that information — whether you work with a functional medicine practitioner, adjust your eating long-term, or bring it to your doctor — is up to you.

Is it right for everyone?

Honestly, no. AIP is strict and it requires planning. If you have a history of disordered eating, it's not the right tool — restriction-based protocols can be harmful in that context.

But for people who have been dismissed, who have symptoms that don't show up on standard tests, who have tried everything and are still not feeling well — AIP is one of the most structured ways to start getting real information about what's happening in your body.

What I'd think about before starting

  • Give it the full elimination phase — 30 days minimum before reintroduction
  • Plan your meals before you start — winging it doesn't work
  • Track your symptoms throughout so reintroduction means something
  • Work with a practitioner if you can — a functional medicine practitioner or dietitian familiar with AIP can make the process less overwhelming
  • Don't start during a high-stress period — your body is already dealing with enough

Your body is telling you something. AIP is one way to start listening carefully enough to hear it.
